Start here

How to use this booklet

This is not a school textbook. It is a kitchen-table guide for real life in Panama: the farmacia, the clinic, the mercado, the taxi, and the neighbor who waters your plants when you fly home to see the grandkids.

Spanish looks like a mountain from far away. Up close, you only need a few paths. This booklet is those paths. Skip around. Dog-ear the pages you actually use. Nobody is giving you a quiz.

A simple way to use it

  1. Skim the first half once so the ideas sound familiar.
  2. Save the web version on your phone. Print the phrase pages if you like paper.
  3. When you get stuck, flip to the pattern you need — not the whole book.
  4. Say the sentences out loud. Spanish gets friendlier once your mouth has practiced it.

If you remember nothing else

Smile. Say por favor and gracias. Use usted with people you don't know well. And the magic phrase: Más despacio, por favor. — A little slower, please.

Panama Spanish, in one paragraph

People here speak Latin American Spanish. They do not use Spain's vosotros. The safe word for “you” with strangers, shopkeepers, doctors, and officials is usted. Friends may switch to tú — follow their lead. And yes, the money is already in dollars. You can skip that particular headache.

What actually trips English speakers

The splits English does not make are the ones that last: two ways to say “to be” (especially estar), two past tenses, and little words like lo and la. Mixing up ser and estar, or using the wrong past, is what most often confuses a listener. A wrong el or la almost never does.

Mistakes are allowed. Panamanians deal with visitors from everywhere, and a kind attempt almost always beats perfect silence. If someone answers you in English, take the help. You can still sneak in a gracias on the way out.

Chapter 2

How Spanish sounds

Here is the best news in this whole booklet: Spanish is mostly phonetic. Once the sounds are in your ear, you can read a menu out loud and be close enough.

The five vowels never really change

English vowels wander all over the place. Spanish vowels stay home. Learn these five and a lot of words suddenly look readable.

Two extra spelling notes: gue and gui have a silent u (guitarra). And qué, cuál, cómo, dónde, cuándo, quién take an accent when they are questions.

Where the stress goes

  • If there is an accent mark, stress that syllable: café, teléfono, Panamá.
  • No accent, and the word ends in a vowel, n, or s? Stress the second-to-last syllable: casa, hablan, lunes.
  • No accent, and it ends in any other consonant? Stress the last syllable: doctor, hotel, español.

Skip the rolled r if you have to

The sound that marks you as English is usually the vowels — keep them short and pure, not “no-uh” or “day-ee.” A weak rolled rr almost never blocks meaning. A swallowed vowel sometimes does.

What your ear will notice here

Along the coast, a final s can sound soft or almost disappear. Más o menos might sound like “mah o meno.” You did not go deaf. That is just Panama talking — and it can hide plurals when you listen. In the highlands (Boquete, Volcán, El Valle) speech is often a bit clearer.

Chapter 3

El, la, and the idea of gender

Every Spanish noun is masculine or feminine. There is no secret logic that makes a table masculine and a chair feminine. Learn the little word in front with the noun, the way you learned “a pair of scissors.” English speakers slip here for years — and listeners still understand them.

MasculineFeminine
theel (plural: los)la (plural: las)
a / anun (plural: unos)una (plural: unas)

A few handy patterns — not rules you must defend in court:

  • Words ending in -o are usually masculine: el banco, el mercado, el baño.
  • Words ending in -a are usually feminine: la casa, la farmacia, la cuenta.
  • Words ending in -ción, -sión, -dad, -tad are feminine: la dirección, la ciudad.
  • When in doubt, English speakers default to masculine. The slips that last are almost always on feminine words. Learn la with those nouns and you cut the problem in half.

Professions: el and la tell you who

Some job words don't change at all. The little word in front is the gender: el for a man, la for a woman. This is the usual pattern for words ending in -ista.

Other jobs do change the ending: el médico / la médica, el vecino / la vecina. Either way, listen for el or la — that is who they are.

Chapter 6

Two ways to say “to be”

English has one “to be.” Spanish has two: ser and estar. English speakers grab ser first and overuse it for years. The work is estar — especially for how someone feels and whether something is open, closed, ready, or broken.

Ser — what something is (this one comes naturally)

Use ser for identity, origin, occupation, time, dates, and the stuff something is made of. If it answers “what is it?” or “who is it?”, you probably want ser.

Soy de Canadá.I'm from Canada.
Soy de los Estados Unidos.I'm from the United States.
Soy jubilado.I'm retired. (a man — that's who you are)
Soy jubilada.I'm retired. (a woman — that's who you are)
Ella es médica.She is a doctor.
Somos vecinos.We're neighbors.
Es el martes.It's Tuesday.
Son las tres.It's three o'clock.
El anillo es de oro.The ring is made of gold.
¿Quién es? — Es el plomero.Who is it? — It's the plumber.

Estar — how or where something is (practice this one)

Location is always estar, even if the place never moves: Panamá está en Centroamérica. Feelings, health, and “how is it right now?” are estar too. That last group is where English speakers keep using ser by accident.

Estoy en David.I'm in David.
Estamos en la capital.We're in Panama City.
¿Cómo está usted? — Estoy bien.How are you? — I'm well.
Estoy cansado.I'm tired. (a man)
Estoy cansada.I'm tired. (a woman)
Estoy enfermo.I'm sick. (a man)
Estoy enferma.I'm sick. (a woman)
El banco está cerrado. La farmacia está abierta.The bank is closed. The pharmacy is open.
El baño está ocupado.The bathroom is occupied.
Está listo.It's ready.
La sopa está caliente. El aire no está frío.The soup is hot (right now). The A/C isn't cold.
Está lloviendo.It's raining.

The mix-up that actually confuses people

A wrong el or la rarely blocks meaning. Picking the wrong “to be” often does. Soy enfermo sounds like “I am a sick person by nature.” Estoy enfermo is “I'm sick (right now).” Soy jubilado is your identity.

A pocket rule

Ser = what it is. Estar = how or where it is right now. If you can add “at the moment” in English, try estar.

Hay — there is / there are

One more cousin: hay (sounds like “eye”). It means there is or there are. It does not change for plural. Mixing up hay with es or está is another mix-up listeners actually feel.

Hay un banco cerca.There is a bank nearby.
¿Hay una farmacia por aquí?Is there a pharmacy around here?
No hay problema.No problem. (you'll hear this all day)
Hay mucha gente.There are a lot of people.

Hay vs. está

Hay un banco en la esquina introduces it — there is a bank. El banco está en la esquina locates a bank you both already have in mind. If you are asking whether something exists nearby, start with ¿Hay…?

Chapter 7

Need a verb in a hurry?

You can often skip conjugating altogether: Quiero + a verb, Necesito + a verb, Voy a + a verb. Chapter 10 is built on that trick.

Hace calor — and then some

Weather uses hacer: Hace calor. Hace sol. Está lloviendo. In Panama, verano is the dry season (roughly December to April) and invierno is the rainy season. Nobody here is talking about snow.

Chapter 9

I like it — the gustar twist

Gustar is not “I like.” It is closer to “it is pleasing to me.” That is why the verb agrees with the thing you like, not with you. Weird for about a week. Then it clicks.

Me gusta el café.I like coffee. (one thing: gusta)
Me gustan las empanadas.I like empanadas. (plural: gustan)
No me gusta el ruido.I don't like the noise.
¿Le gusta el pescado?Do you like fish? (usted)
Nos gusta Panamá.We like Panama.
WordEnglish
meto me
teto you (tú)
leto you (usted) / to him / to her
nosto us
lesto you all / to them

Want to make it extra clear? Add a phrase up front: A mí me gusta… A usted le gusta… You'll hear that a lot when people contrast tastes.

Don't say yo gusto

English wants “I like.” Spanish will not take Yo gusto el café. Start with me: Me gusta el café. Same family: Me duele aquí. Me encanta este lugar. Me falta dinero.

Love it? Same pattern: Me encanta este lugar. Me encantan las islas. That is the friendly way to say “I love it” about food, towns, and weather — not usually about people. For people, Quiero mucho a… is safer.

Your future tense, for now

Spanish has a “real” future tense (hablaré, irá). You can ignore it for months. Voy a… is what people actually say about later today, tomorrow, and next Tuesday.

Chapter 11

Yesterday, without the headache

Spanish has two everyday past tenses. English has one. That is why this chapter lasts for years. You need a snapshot version: one past for finished events, one past for background and “used to.” The English trap is using the snapshot past for both.

The snapshot: something finished

Use this when the thing happened and it's done. Yesterday. This morning. Last Tuesday. A completed trip to the clinic.

Ayer fui al médico.Yesterday I went to the doctor.
Comí pescado.I ate fish.
Pagamos en efectivo.We paid in cash.
¿Qué pasó?What happened?

The English trap

If you can add “used to” or “was …-ing” in English, do not use the snapshot past. Comí pescado is “I ate fish (and I was done).” Comía pescado is “I used to eat fish / I was eating fish.” English speakers keep putting the snapshot where the background belongs. Listeners feel that one.

Ayer comí pescado.Yesterday I ate fish. (done)
Cuando vivía en Florida, comía pescado.When I lived in Florida, I used to eat fish.
Fui al médico el martes.I went to the doctor on Tuesday. (that visit)
Iba al médico cada mes.I used to go to the doctor every month.

If you freeze, tell the story in two strokes

Background first, snapshot second: Estaba en la casa y se fue la luz. I was at the house and the power went out. That pattern will carry a lot of stories.

How they're built, if you're curious

Usted: start with the yo form in the present, drop the -o, and add the “opposite” vowel — hablo to hable, como to coma, vivo to viva. Rebels: tenga, venga, diga, haga, ponga, vaya, sea, esté, dé. Tú yes-commands are usually the he/she present: habla, come, vive. Rebels: di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven. Tú no-commands look like usted plus an s: no fumes, no hables.

Regáleme un vaso de agua

You will hear regalar used for “hand me / give me,” not only for birthday presents. ¿Me regala una bolsa? is a normal way to ask for a bag at the checkout. With a friend: Regálame una bolsa. They are not asking you to donate one.

Chapter 13

Por and para — the short version

Both can translate as “for,” which is unhelpful. You do not need every textbook list. You need two jobs for para and three jobs for por.

Para — purpose, destination, deadline, recipient

Este café es para usted.This coffee is for you.
Salimos para Boquete el viernes.We're leaving for Boquete on Friday.
Lo necesito para mañana.I need it by tomorrow.
Estudio para hablar mejor.I study in order to speak better.

Gracias por su ayuda.Thank you for your help.
Caminamos por la playa.We walked along the beach.

Pagué diez dólares por esto.I paid ten dollars for this.

Stuck?

If it means “in order to,” “by this date,” “headed to,” or “this is for that person,” try para. If it means “because of,” “through,” “for a stretch of time,” or “in exchange for,” try por. And memorize por favor as a chunk. Do not take it apart.

Ahorita is a lifestyle

Ahorita can mean right now, in a few minutes, or later this afternoon, depending on the smile that comes with it. If you need a real time, ask: ¿A qué hora, más o menos? Around what time?

A few tiny extras you'll hear constantly

  • -ito / -ita makes things smaller or kinder: momentito, cafecito, abuelita. It is friendly, not baby talk.
  • lo and la often mean “it” or “him/her,” and they sit in front of the verb: ¿Lo vio? Did you see it? La tengo. I have it. If that throws you, just repeat the noun. English speakers trip here more than on verbs — recognition first is enough.
  • You'll hear se lo a lot: Se lo dije. I told him/her it. You do not have to build this yet. Just know it is not a new verb.
  • se also shows up on signs: Se vende. Se alquila. Se habla español. For sale. For rent. Spanish spoken here.
  • Me llamo is technically a reflexive verb. So is Me siento mal (I feel bad). Learn them as phrases.
